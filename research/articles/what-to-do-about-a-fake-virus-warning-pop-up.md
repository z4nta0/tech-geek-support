You're browsing the web, and suddenly the screen fills with a loud, flashing warning: "Your computer is infected!" or "Microsoft Security Alert: call this number immediately." Sometimes there's even a voice or a beeping alarm, and the window won't close.

It's frightening, and it's designed to be. But here's the most important thing to know:

**Real virus warnings never ask you to call a phone number.** This is a scam, often called a **tech support scam**. Your computer is almost certainly fine. The page is just a website pretending to be a warning.

## What NOT to do

- **Don't call the number.** The people who answer pretend to be Microsoft, Apple, or another company. They'll try to get into your computer and get your money.
- **Don't click anything inside the warning**, even buttons that say "Close" or "Cancel."
- **Don't let anyone take control of your computer** from a call or pop-up you didn't expect.
- **Never pay** anyone with gift cards, a wire transfer, cryptocurrency, or a payment app because of a warning like this. Real companies don't ask for that.

## How to close the warning on Windows

These pages often fill the whole screen so you can't find the close button. You can close the web browser using **Task Manager**, a built-in Windows tool.

1. Right-click the **Start** button (the Windows logo) and click **Task Manager**. (If the warning covers the Start button, press **Ctrl + Shift + Esc** on your keyboard instead, all three keys together. That opens Task Manager directly.)
2. On the left side, click **Processes**.
3. Under **Apps**, right-click your web browser (for example, **Microsoft Edge** or **Google Chrome**) and click **End task**.
4. Check that the browser has closed, then close Task Manager.

[Image: Task Manager on Windows 11 with Microsoft Edge right-clicked and "End task" highlighted]

When you open your browser again, if it offers to reopen or "restore" your last pages, don't. Start fresh.

## How to close the warning on a Mac

1. Press **Option + Command + Esc** together. A small **Force Quit** window opens.
2. Click your web browser (such as **Safari** or **Google Chrome**).
3. Click **Force Quit**.

You can also open the **Apple** menu in the top-left corner and choose **Force Quit**.

## After it's closed: check your computer

To be safe, run a security scan.

- **On Windows 11:** Click **Start**, then **Settings**, then **Privacy & security**, then **Windows Security**, then **Virus & threat protection**, and click **Quick scan**.
- **On a Mac or any computer with security software:** make sure the software is up to date and run a scan.

> **Tip:** These warnings often come from a mistyped web address or a misleading ad. If one keeps appearing on a site you visit often, try typing the address more carefully or using a bookmark.

## If you already called or let someone in

It's okay. Many smart, careful people have been fooled by these scams. Act quickly and calmly:

1. **Hang up or disconnect.** If they're still connected to your computer, turn the computer off.
2. **Paid with a credit or debit card?** Call the number on the back of your card. Tell them it was a scam and ask them to reverse the charge.
3. **Paid with a gift card?** Call the company that issued the card (the number is on the back). Tell them a scammer tricked you, and ask for a refund. Keep the card and the receipt.
4. **Remove anything they had you install.** Then run a full security scan.
5. **Change your passwords**, starting with your email and bank, from a different, safe device if you can. Change any other account that used the same password.
6. **Report it** to the Federal Trade Commission at **ReportFraud.ftc.gov**. Reporting helps stop these scammers.

> **Before you start:** If someone had remote control of your computer, it's worth having a trusted person or repair shop check it over, or resetting the computer. Ask us if you're not sure.

## If that didn't work

- **The pop-up comes back every time you open the browser?** When you reopen it, don't restore the previous pages. If it still appears, the browser's settings may have been changed. A trusted helper can reset them.
- **Worried about a phone call instead of a pop-up?** The same rule applies: Microsoft and Apple don't make surprise support calls. Hang up.

## Research notes (not published)

- **Why this problem**: Tech support scams are among the costliest scams for older adults. Coverage of the FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Complaint Center data reports adults 60 and older lost about $7.7 billion overall, with tech support scams listed among the costliest categories and over $2.1 billion in tech support losses across all ages. The fake pop-up is the most common entry point, and it is exactly the kind of moment a reader would search for help.
- **Where it was found**:
  - [AARP: FBI and FTC report on 2025 losses](https://www.aarp.org/money/scams-fraud/fbi-ftc-report-2025-losses): older adults' losses and the costliest categories.
  - [Connected Nation: Scams are becoming smarter](https://connectednation.org/blog/scams-are-becoming-smarter-heres-how-you-can-outsmart-them): the $2.1 billion tech support figure.
  - [Bitdefender: Older adults lost billions to scammers last year, FBI warns](https://www.bitdefender.com/en-us/blog/hotforsecurity/older-adults-lost-billions-to-scammers-2026)
  - Limitation: the FBI's IC3 report itself was not read; figures come from secondary coverage and are kept out of the published article for that reason. No r/Scams thread counts were gathered.
- **Sources for the steps**:
  - [FTC: How to spot, avoid, and report tech support scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-spot-avoid-and-report-tech-support-scams): verified "Real security pop-up warnings and messages will never ask you to call a phone number," don't call, payment methods scammers demand, contacting your card company to reverse charges, changing passwords, reporting at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
  - [FTC: What to do if you were scammed](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-do-if-you-were-scammed): verified the gift card steps (call the number on the back, ask for a refund, keep the card and receipt), and remote-access steps (update security software, scan, change passwords).
  - [Microsoft Support: Protect yourself from tech support scams](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/protect-yourself-from-tech-support-scams-2ebf91bd-f94c-2a8a-e541-f5c800d18435): verified no unsolicited calls, error messages never include phone numbers, uninstall what scammers installed, consider resetting, run a scan, change passwords, contest charges.
